# Laughed At, Proven Right — Scripts 1–3

Nov 20, 2026 · Rami

Three 60–75 second Shorts built on the Ricks Clips formula: strange opening → "You see…" → everyone doubts him → the easy way seems to win → warning signs → the low point → payoff and how it works. No call to action, so it loops. Each one is about 230–260 words for an AI voice at a steady storyteller pace. The stickman beats in brackets are for OMS Studio.

**Accuracy note:** every key fact is real. The lines in quotes are dramatized crowd reactions, not quotes from real people, so don't put them in a named person's mouth on screen.

## Script 1 — The Stone That Said "Don't Build Here"

**Title:** The Stone That Said "Don't Build Here"
**Alt titles:** Why This Village Survived the Tsunami · The Warning Nobody Read

He carved one sentence into a stone and set it halfway up a mountain. *Do not build your homes below this point.* [Old stickman chiselling a stone on a hillside]

You see, this was a tiny fishing village in Japan, and the sea had already wiped it out. Twice. In 1896, only two people survived. In 1933, only four. So the survivors put up the stone and moved every house above it. [Two waves sweep a village; then a few stickmen carrying a house uphill]

But up there, the walk to the boats is long and steep. Every single morning. Uphill, downhill, carrying nets. And down by the water, the flat land is just sitting there. Easy. Other towns along the coast build right on the shore, behind big new sea walls. They call it progress. [Tired stickman with nets on a steep path; a shiny town behind a wall below]

Years pass. The people who saw the waves grow old and pass away. Moss covers the stone. Kids walk past it to school without even reading it. "Why live on a mountain when the sea has a wall?" [Mossy stone, kids walking past, one shrugs]

Then, March 11th, 2011. The ground shakes for minutes. Down the coast, the sea walls were built for a big wave. This one is bigger. It pours over them like they aren't there. [Wave spilling over a wall]

It rushes up the valley toward the little village. Past the fields. Past the road. Still climbing. And it stops about 300 feet below the stone. [Water creeping uphill, slowing, stopping; camera pans up to the stone]

Every house above that line was still standing. Because the people who'd seen it twice wrote down exactly where the water ends. [Villagers standing beside the stone looking down]

**Fact check:** Aneyoshi, Iwate Prefecture. The stone went up after the 1933 tsunami. The survivor counts (2 in 1896, 4 in 1933) and the 2011 wave stopping about 300 feet below the stone come from the widely reported 2011 coverage (e.g. AP/NYT). Read the inscription in the voiceover as a paraphrase.

## Script 2 — The Doctor Who Drank the Bacteria

**Title:** The Doctor Who Drank the Bacteria
**Alt titles:** Why He Gave Himself a Disease · Everyone Said Nothing Lives in Your Stomach

He poured a glass full of bacteria and drank it in one gulp. [Stickman in a lab coat raising a murky glass]

You see, in the early 1980s, every doctor knew what caused stomach ulcers. Stress. Spicy food. Too much acid. Patients were told to relax, drink milk, and take pills, sometimes for the rest of their lives. [Doctor handing a patient a huge bottle of pills]

But a young doctor in Australia, Barry Marshall, and his colleague Robin Warren kept finding the same thing in sick stomachs: a tiny spiral-shaped bacterium. Marshall said it wasn't stress. It was an infection, and it could be cured. [Microscope view of a spiral germ]

The experts didn't buy it. Nothing can live in stomach acid. That's like a fish living in a fire. His work got rejected, and at conferences people rolled their eyes. [Audience of stickmen laughing and rolling their eyes]

He tried to infect pigs to prove it. Didn't work. He couldn't test it on patients. So there was only one stomach left. His own. [Confused pig; Marshall looks at his own belly]

He didn't even tell his wife. He drank it. For a few days, nothing. Then the vomiting started. His breath turned foul. About ten days in, a colleague looked inside his stomach and found it inflamed and crawling with the exact bacteria he'd swallowed. [Green-faced stickman; scope image full of spirals]

He'd given himself the illness the whole world said couldn't happen. Then he took antibiotics, and it cleared up. [Stickman swallowing a pill, colour returning]

Today, ulcers caused by that bug are cured with a short course of antibiotics instead of a lifetime of pills. And in 2005, the two men nobody believed won the Nobel Prize. [Two stickmen holding medals]

**Fact check:** Self-experiment in 1984. The failed pig trials, not telling his wife, symptoms within about a week, endoscopy around day 10 showing gastritis and *H. pylori*, and the 2005 Nobel shared with Robin Warren are all on record. He developed gastritis, not a full ulcer, so the script says "illness" on purpose. Don't change it to "gave himself an ulcer."

## Script 3 — The Pump Handle That Beat Cholera

**Title:** The Pump Handle That Beat Cholera
**Alt titles:** Why a Doctor Asked London to Break Its Pump · The Map That Found a Killer

He walked up to a water pump in the middle of London and asked the city to take its handle off. [Stickman in a top hat pointing at an old street pump]

You see, it's 1854, and cholera is tearing through Soho. Over 500 people dead in ten days. And everyone knows what causes it: bad air. A foul smell rising from the filth in the streets. So people hold handkerchiefs over their noses and keep their windows shut. [Green fog in the street, stickmen covering their faces]

But a doctor named John Snow doesn't buy it. If it's the air, why is it only hitting the people who drink from one pump on Broad Street? [Snow squinting at the pump]

He goes door to door with a notebook. Every death becomes a mark on his map, and the marks crowd around that pump like flies. Then come the strange cases. A workhouse just around the corner, hundreds of people, hardly any deaths. They had their own well. A brewery on the same street, and not one worker dies. They drink beer, not water. [Map filling with black bars; brewery workers cheering with mugs]

He takes his map to the officials. The water looks clean. It tastes fine. Something invisible in water killing people? Ridiculous. But they agree to take the handle off anyway, just in case. [Officials shrugging; a worker unscrewing the handle]

The outbreak was already fading, and an official inquiry later rejected his theory. Bad air, they said. [Stamp: REJECTED]

Then a local priest, digging through the cases, found where it all began: a sick baby's nappies, rinsed into a cesspit just three feet from the well. The pump had been drinking from the sewer. [Cutaway diagram: cesspit leaking into the well]

John Snow was right. And that one missing handle helped change how cities protect their drinking water. [Handle-less pump, clean water tap fade-in]

**Fact check:** The 1854 Broad Street outbreak (500+ deaths in about 10 days), the Poland Street workhouse with its own well and few deaths, the Broad Street brewery with no worker deaths, the handle removed on 8 September after the parish Board of Guardians met, the 1855 Board of Health rejecting the water theory, and Rev. Henry Whitehead tracing the index case (an infant's soiled nappies, a cesspool about 3 ft from the well) are all on record.

## Production notes

- **Pace:** about 3.7 words a second. If a render goes past 75 s, cut a sentence from the "years pass" or "strange cases" beats first, never from the payoff.
- **Last line:** stop the music hard on the final sentence and cut straight to black, so it loops into the opening line.
- **On-screen text:** only the key word of each beat ("1896", "REJECTED", "300 FT"). Keep the frames clean.
- **Order to post:** Script 1 first (the strongest picture), then 3, then 2.
